O﻿n review sites, especially those for books, ratings tend to be set to stars, with five stars being \*perfection\*. And while this might be fine for objective rankings based on imperical evidence, opinions are not definitive, but based on personal preference. Which is why, to remind reviewers and readers that these are subjective, books should be ranked with hearts instead of stars.

I﻿ have been using heart rankings in my book journal for just over a year now. When I set up my A6 notebook, I made a little key to remind myself what my standards to ensure consistency. One heart was a DNF or hating it; two was a book I disliked, or just felt was forgettable; three meant it was just ok; four if I liked it and would reread it (which I do often); and five was for books I loved and new favourites. My book choices tended towards the three to five range, thankfully, but by making it clear in these rules about my own personal use, I felt it was clearer that these ratings were opinion based.

Platforms like The StoryGraph or Goodreads or social media, the ranking is shown in the stars. In [hospitality settings](https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/guides/zvjm47h/revision/1), this is to help customers plan according to standards and budget. The famed [Michelin stars](https://guide.michelin.com/us/en/article/features/what-is-a-michelin-star) are assigned based on five criteria set by the organisation. But with books, reviewers can be anybody going by any standard, and are therefore more of a popularity contest. What one reader considers the most important aspect of the text can be meaningless to another, making the way they are assessed completely random and subjective. Yet still people talk about five star books as if that will be a universal experience.
